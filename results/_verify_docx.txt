[P] Abstract
[P] Background/Objectives: Disability, multimorbidity and depression co-occur and may share upstream determinants. Whether their cumulative burden predicts incident dementia independently of reverse causation, and how much of any effect is channelled through depression, remain unresolved.
[P] Methods: We pooled six harmonized ageing cohorts (CHARLS, ELSA, HRS, KLoSA, MHAS, SHARE). The exposure was an unweighted three-component index (0–3) of disability, multimorbidity and depression; incident dementia was ascertained algorithmically. Cohort-specific Cox models adjusted for sociodemographic and lifestyle covariates were pooled by DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis; time-lagged, time-varying, trajectory, competing-risk and E-value analyses were fitted, and mediation was decomposed under three frameworks.
[P] Results: Among 71,867 participants with complete exposure data (11,185 incident dementia cases; 694,305 person-years), the primary model included 66,765 (10,297 events). Each additional syndrome was associated with a 27 % higher risk of dementia (pooled HR 1.27, 95 % CI 1.21–1.33; I² = 76.8 %), with a monotonic gradient in all six cohorts; depression contributed most (1.50) and multimorbidity least (1.16). Excluding the first two years left the estimate unchanged, with no attenuation as the lag increased (P = 0.128); exposure measured four to six years before outcome ascertainment gave 1.33, and requiring the criterion at two consecutive assessments gave 1.21 (1.13–1.30). The proportion mediated by depression converged at 12.7–17.4 %.
[P] Conclusions: Cumulative geriatric syndrome burden is robustly and dose-dependently associated with incident dementia, survives deliberate time-lagging, and is partly channelled through depression.
[P] 1. Introduction
[P] Dementia is among the leading causes of dependency and death in older adults, and the number of people living with dementia worldwide is projected to rise steeply as populations age [1,2]. Because no disease-modifying treatment is yet available, identifying modifiable upstream targets remains the central preventive strategy. The 2024 Lancet Commission identified 14 modifiable risk factors and estimated that nearly half of all dementia cases could in principle be prevented or delayed by addressing them [1]. Notably, several of those factors — in particular depression, physical inactivity and diabetes — are themselves manifestations of the same underlying vulnerability that produces the classic geriatric syndromes.
[P] Geriatric syndromes are defined as "multifactorial health conditions that occur when the accumulated effects of impairments in multiple systems render an older person vulnerable to situational challenges" [3]. The defining feature is multifactorial aetiology and shared risk factors rather than a single causal pathway [3,4]. Disability, multimorbidity and depression are three of the most common and most readily ascertained members of this family, and each has been associated with subsequent dementia [5–13]. Critically, however, they cluster: the same older adult frequently has several of them, and the co-occurrence is not incidental but a consequence of shared upstream determinants. Studying them one at a time therefore risks misattributing to a single syndrome an effect that belongs to the aggregate burden.
[P] Two recent studies have taken that combination as their exposure. Du and colleagues pooled the Health and Retirement Study (HRS 2012–2018) and SHARE (2012–2018), 14,482 participants in total, and reported that those carrying both a physical disorder — any one of seven self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions — and above-threshold depressive symptoms had 2.15 times the dementia risk of those carrying neither (95 % CI 1.27–3.03) [14]. Ding and colleagues examined physical frailty and depression jointly in the UK Biobank, ELSA and HRS, and reported a pooled hazard ratio of 3.23 (2.86–3.65) for the combination, together with a statistically significant additive interaction [15]. Both analyses rest on two or three cohorts drawn from the United States, the United Kingdom and continental Europe; their physical component is either the presence of any one of seven reported diagnoses or a frailty phenotype built from measured physical performance; neither includes disability, the functional limitation that the geriatric-syndrome literature treats as central [3]; neither tests whether the association survives a deliberate separation of exposure from outcome; and neither decomposes the effect into direct and depression-mediated components.
[P] Accumulation-based indices have proved productive in the closely related frailty literature. Ward and colleagues examined frailty trajectories in four US and UK cohorts and found that frailty remained positively associated with dementia even when measured well before the prodromal acceleration period [5]; Luo and colleagues reported that progression in frailty increased and reversal decreased dementia risk [6]. A parallel literature has defined the sequence in which chronic conditions accumulate over the life course and shown that dementia risk can be stratified by both the type and the timing of the accumulated conditions [16]. These studies establish the value of a cumulative construct, but they rely on frailty indices computed from 30 or more deficits, or on physical frailty phenotypes requiring grip strength and gait speed — measurements that are missing in much of the world's ageing survey infrastructure and are impractical in routine primary care.
[P] The counting logic itself is already established in geriatric practice. A prospective multicentre study counted 14 geriatric syndromes at admission across 43 hospitals on three continents and found that each additional syndrome was associated with a 22 % higher 90-day mortality (HR 1.22, 95 % CI 1.15–1.30) [17]. That work validated the prognostic content of an unweighted syndrome count at scale, but its outcome was death rather than dementia and its setting was acute hospital care; whether a comparable count assembled from routine community survey items predicts cognitive decline has not been tested.
[P] Four questions remain open, and each extends rather than repeats the work above. First, does the association hold for a minimal accumulation index assembled only from items that every major ageing survey already collects, and does it replicate beyond the North American, British and continental European cohorts studied so far? Second, is the association a long-term risk relation or a manifestation of pre-clinical disease, and does it survive designs that deliberately place the exposure several years before outcome ascertainment? Third, is depression merely a co-manifestation, or does it carry a substantial share of the effect of physical syndromes? Fourth, do physical and psychological burden act independently, or does one amplify the other [15]?
[P] We addressed these questions in 71,867 participants from six population-based cohorts on three continents — to our knowledge the first multicohort analysis of this exposure construct to include both East Asian and Latin American populations — using a harmonized three-component index and a deliberately redundant analytic strategy: multiple dose–response specifications, mutually adjusted components, all eight combinations of the three syndromes, a cumulative-lag design, an interval-based time-varying design with up to two-wave exposure–outcome separation, exposure-trajectory and level-versus-change models, competing-risk analysis, and mediation quantified under three independent identification frameworks.
[P] 2. Materials and Methods
[P] 2.1. Study population
[P] We used six population-based longitudinal cohorts of ageing harmonized through the Gateway to Global Aging Data platform [18]: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [19], the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) [20], the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [21], the Korean Longitudinal Study of Ageing (KLoSA) [22], the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) [23] and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) [24]. Together these cohorts cover populations in Asia, Europe and North America. Follow-up is biennial in all cohorts, except that MHAS had a longer gap between its second and third waves, and HRS and KLoSA begin from mid-life. All use comparable computer-assisted instruments for chronic conditions, functional limitations and depressive symptoms.
[P] The analytic sample was derived from 103,444 baseline respondents (Figure 1, Table S2). We required: an interpretable baseline cognitive assessment; absence of dementia at baseline; age 50–105 years; survival to and cognitive assessment during follow-up; and complete data on all three exposure components. Successive exclusions left 71,867 participants. Of these, 66,765 (10,297 events) had complete data on all model covariates and formed the primary analysis sample.

[P] Wave structures differ across cohorts, and baseline wave selection was determined empirically to maximize the availability of the exposure and outcome constructs: CHARLS wave 1 (2011), ELSA wave 1 (2002), HRS wave 5 (2000), KLoSA wave 3 (2010), MHAS wave 3 (2012) and SHARE wave 1 (2004). Deaths were identified from cohort-specific vital-status files. Because the Indian cohort (LASI) has only a single wave, it could not support an incident-event structure and was excluded from all primary analyses.
[P] All cohorts obtained informed consent and ethical approval from their own governing bodies; we analysed de-identified public-use data and therefore required no additional approval.
[P] 2.2. Exposure: a three-component geriatric syndrome accumulation index (DAI)
[P] We constructed the DAI as the unweighted count (range 0–3) of three geriatric syndromes present at the baseline wave:
[P] Disability — self-reported difficulty with at least one basic activity of daily living (bathing, dressing, eating, transferring from bed, toileting and walking, as available in each cohort) [25] or at least one instrumental activity of daily living (preparing meals, shopping, using the telephone, managing money, taking medication and others, as available) [26]. A component was set to positive if any available item was endorsed, requiring at least 60 % of the items in a set to be non-missing.
[P] Following the harmonized files, the Katz items are reproduced as bathing, dressing, eating, transferring from bed, toileting and walking; the harmonized instrument therefore substitutes walking for the continence item of the original Katz index, and walking was retained because it is the item measured consistently in every cohort.
[P] Multimorbidity — two or more self-reported, physician-diagnosed chronic conditions from a set of seven harmonized conditions (hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke, arthritis) [27].
[P] Depression and cognitive impairment are deliberately excluded from these seven conditions: depression is a separate component of the index, and cognitive impairment is the outcome construct, so including either would build the exposure out of the outcome domain. The number was fixed at seven because these are the conditions ascertained identically across all six cohorts.
[P] Depression — a depressive-symptom score above a cohort-specific validated threshold, using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) in CHARLS and KLoSA, the 8-item CES-D in ELSA and HRS, the 9-item CES-D in MHAS and the 12-item EURO-D scale in SHARE [28,29].
[P] A participant received a DAI score only if all three components were non-missing. Candidate components were pre-screened for cross-cohort availability and directional consistency before the index was finalized (Table S1); falls, urinary incontinence, self-rated health, sleep and sensory measures were evaluated and rejected because they were either incomparable in recall period, split in direction across cohorts, or already incorporated in existing measures. The unweighted counting approach follows the deficit-accumulation tradition [4] and its recent application to geriatric syndromes counted in hospital practice [17]; it was preferred to a weighted index because it can be computed at the point of care without a scoring algorithm. Component directions were verified cohort by cohort so that a higher score always denotes more deficit.
[P] 2.3. Outcome: incident dementia
[P] The primary outcome was incident all-cause dementia, defined as the first wave at which the participant met a harmonized algorithmic criterion for probable dementia. The criterion was an education-stratified global cognitive z-score at or below −1.5. The global score was the mean of the available domain scores (memory, executive function, orientation and verbal fluency), each standardized against a reference distribution fixed across waves and taken from the earliest wave with at least 500 observations in the age band 50–69 years; fixing the reference preserves within-person decline rather than normalizing it away. Because the instrument battery is not identical in every wave, the set of domains entering the global score was fixed within each cohort to those measured consistently from the baseline wave onward (Section S4.1 of the Supplementary Materials). This criterion generalizes the published 25-point rule for these cohorts to every cognitive item available at every wave. Participants meeting the criterion at baseline were excluded; those never meeting it were censored at their last cognitive assessment or at death.
[P] To guard against the possibility that results depend on the specific algorithmic definition, we repeated the primary model using four alternative outcome definitions: the same criterion without education stratification; the published 25-point rule (word recall 0–20 plus serial sevens 0–5, below the education-specific mean minus 1.5 SD) [30]; the cohort-specific classifications distributed with the harmonized files, that is the Langa–Weir algorithm in HRS [30,31], the 25-point algorithmic classification in ELSA, and the cognitive-total classification in KLoSA; and a conservative rule censoring all participants at their last available cognitive assessment.
[P] 2.4. Covariates
[P] Covariates were selected a priori and harmonized as previously described: age and age², sex, education (low / medium / high), marital status (partnered vs not), smoking (never / former / current), current alcohol use, and body mass index. A second model additionally adjusted for urban versus rural residence (not collected in ELSA), and a third for grip strength and physical activity. Because grip strength and gait speed are missing in 5–100 % of these cohorts (never measured in ELSA, HRS or MHAS), the model containing them was estimable in only three cohorts and was treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than a primary specification. Baseline cognitive performance is likewise not a covariate of the primary model, and this was deliberate: the outcome criterion is a threshold on the same global score, and entry required that score to lie above the threshold, so the variable is simultaneously the measurement instrument and the conditioning variable of the sampling frame. Adjusting for it would condition on the selection rule, would partially adjust away a relation that may run from the same underlying vulnerability to both cognition and the syndromes, and — as we verified — is numerically fragile: entering the score continuously destabilised the fit in one cohort, a direct sign of the collinearity created by conditioning on the entry criterion. We nevertheless fitted the model with the score entered as cohort-specific quartile indicators, which keeps all six cohorts estimable. Because the score is standardised within education strata, we also ran the same model with the continuous score and without the education terms.
[P] 2.5. Statistical analysis
[P] Main models. Cohort-specific hazard ratios (HRs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated with Cox proportional-hazards models, with the DAI entered continuously (per one additional syndrome) and categorically (1, 2 or 3 syndromes vs 0). Estimates were pooled by DerSimonian–Laird random-effects meta-analysis [32], and between-cohort heterogeneity was quantified with the I² statistic [33]. Four nested models were fitted: unadjusted (M1); adjusted for age, age², sex, education, marital status, smoking, alcohol use and BMI (M2, primary); M2 plus urban–rural residence (M3); and M2 plus grip strength and physical activity (M4). Proportional-hazards assumptions were checked with scaled Schoenfeld residuals.
[P] Component and joint contributions. Each component was entered alone (M2), then all three were entered simultaneously to assess mutual adjustment and to determine whether any association reversed. We then fitted all eight mutually exclusive combinations of the three syndromes against a common reference of none, to test whether the three syndromes were exchangeable or whether specific pairs carried disproportionate risk. Discrimination was compared between the DAI and a model containing the three dichotomous components, using Harrell's C, and an alternative weighted index (first principal component) was fitted for comparison.
[P] Time-lag and time-varying designs. To test whether the association reflected pre-clinical disease rather than a long-term risk relation, we (i) excluded the first L years of follow-up for L = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 and tested for a linear trend in the log HR; and (ii) constructed counting-process (interval) data in which each participant contributes one row per inter-wave interval [wave k, wave k+1), with the exposure taken from wave k and the outcome evaluated at wave k+1. We fitted three interval models: exposure one wave before outcome assessment; exposure lagged by two waves (4–6 years before outcome assessment); and the cumulative mean of all exposure measurements up to the start of the interval.
[P] Exposure trajectories. We classified participants by their exposure at the baseline and immediately subsequent waves into persistently low (0→0), remitted (1→0), incident (0→1) and persistently affected (1→1), with the exposure window and the outcome window non-overlapping so that change itself could not be a manifestation of the outcome. We reported dementia incidence density per 1,000 person-years by trajectory and pooled HRs for each trajectory against persistently low as the single reference. In a separate parameterization we entered the baseline level and the follow-up level simultaneously — rather than level and change, which are collinear by construction — to separate the contribution of the current level of burden from that of the rate of change.
[P] The trajectory contrast is deliberately dichotomous — any burden (index ≥ 1) versus none — which prioritises the interpretability of reversal over dose resolution; the continuous and categorical models retain the full 0–3 granularity, and the trajectory analysis should be read as a complement to them. Incidence density within a trajectory is reported per unit of person-time at risk in that group; because the groups differ in follow-up length and in mortality, the corresponding hazard ratios, not the densities, are the primary quantity, and the densities are descriptive.
[P] Mediation by depression. We decomposed the effect of non-depressive burden (disability and/or multimorbidity) on dementia into a direct and a depression-mediated component using three independent strategies, deliberately chosen so that agreement between them would not be an artefact of any single set of assumptions:
[P] Cross-sectional difference method. Mediator and exposure measured at the same wave; the mediated proportion was computed as (βtotal − βdirect)/βtotal on the log-HR scale.
[P] Temporally ordered difference method. Exposure at wave 0, mediator at wave 1 (one assessment wave later), outcome followed from wave 1 onward, so that the exposure precedes the mediator. A second variant required the outcome to be assessed from wave 2 onward (two-wave washout), and a third restricted the sample to participants free of depression at wave 0 to isolate incident depression.
[P] Counterfactual g-formula. We computed E[Y(x, M(x′))] on the additive risk scale, with the mediator distribution from a logistic model and 5-year dementia risk from the Cox model, and performed the four-way decomposition into controlled direct effect, reference interaction, mediated interaction and pure indirect effect [34,35]. Confidence intervals were obtained by cohort-stratified bootstrap resampling.
[P] For every mediated proportion we applied a scale-free stability guard: a cohort contributed to the pooled proportion only if its own total effect was large enough for the ratio to be bounded — at least 0.03 in absolute value on the log-hazard scale, and, in the counterfactual framework, at least 0.002 on the risk-difference scale as well. Significance was not used as the criterion, because precision and estimability are different properties: a rule based on the absolute risk scale discards cohorts whose baseline risk is low even though their proportion is well defined. In this analysis the guard excluded no cohort in any framework: the smallest cohort-level total effect on the log-hazard scale was 0.106 (CHARLS), well above the threshold; the rule is described because it is part of the pre-specified procedure, and where fewer than two cohorts had qualified the proportion would have been reported as not poolable rather than pooled.
[P] Additional analyses. We fitted Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models treating death as a competing event, and estimated 10-year cumulative incidence functions by the Aalen–Johansen estimator [36]. We computed E-values for the continuous and the three-syndrome contrasts to quantify the strength that unmeasured confounding would need to attain to explain the results away [37]. We assessed region-level heterogeneity (East Asia, Europe, North America) and performed cohort-level random-effects meta-regression. Leave-one-cohort-out analyses assessed whether any single cohort drove the pooled estimate. Alternative exposure definitions (ADL limitation alone and IADL limitation alone) were examined (Table 3A). To allow direct comparison with published interaction analyses, we converted the counterfactual four-cell risks to the risk-ratio scale and computed the relative excess risk due to interaction, the attributable proportion and the synergy index, together with the ratio of the joint risk ratio to the product of the two single-exposure risk ratios; these were calculated in every cohort in which the counterfactual model was estimable.
[P] Missing data. The exposure was defined only when all three components were non-missing and the other entry criteria were met, which left 71,867 participants. The remaining 7.1 % difference between these participants and the 66,765 with a complete covariate set was dominated by body mass index, which was missing in 13.8 % of CHARLS, 13.1 % of ELSA and 10.2 % of MHAS participants and in under 2.1 % elsewhere (Table S4a). Because participants with missing covariates were older and more often disabled than those with complete data (mean age 64.8 vs 64.0 years; disability 26.0 % vs 17.9 %), missingness was not assumed to be completely at random. Multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 20) was therefore performed under two schemes: imputing covariates only, and imputing covariates together with the three exposure components. Continuous variables were imputed from a linear model with posterior draws of the coefficients and the residual variance, and binary variables from a logistic model with posterior draws of the coefficients followed by Bernoulli sampling; the predictor set included the event indicator and log follow-up time [38]. Estimates were combined within cohort by Rubin's rules and pooled across cohorts by the same random-effects procedure as the primary analysis.
[P] Subgroup and interaction analyses. Six effect modifiers were prespecified: age group (< 65, 65–74, ≥ 75 years), sex, education (low / medium / high), baseline cognition (below or above the cohort-specific median of the harmonized global score), marital status and body mass index category. For each modifier we pooled stratum-specific hazard ratios within stratum and tested multiplicative interaction by pooling the cohort-specific interaction coefficients from a model containing the index, the modifier and their product. To preserve full rank, the covariate set corresponding to the modifier was removed from that model — the continuous and quadratic age terms when stratifying by age, the sex indicator when stratifying by sex, and so on. Multiplicity across the six modifiers was addressed by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, as described below, and the interaction results are presented as exploratory.
[P] Sample-size note for the time-varying analyses. Participants contributing only one cognitive assessment wave cannot enter a counting-process model; we quantified and reported the baseline characteristics of those excluded.
[P] All analyses were performed in Python 3.13 with pandas, NumPy, lifelines and statsmodels. Cohort-specific models used a small penalizer (10⁻⁴) on the baseline hazard for numerical stability; subset models with sparse event times were additionally fitted at a heavier penalizer (10⁻²) and discarded if the log HR moved by more than 0.10, a safeguard adopted after sparse-event subsets were found to produce unstable estimates. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Hazard ratios are reported to two decimal places in the text and tables and to three decimals in the figure annotations, where the additional digit separates adjacent cohort estimates.
[P] This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cohort studies (see the completed checklist in the Supplementary Materials).
[P] Use of AI-assisted tools. AI-assisted tools were used for code generation, statistical analysis support and language editing. All analyses were designed, verified and interpreted by the authors; every numerical result, table and figure was produced by the authors' own analysis pipeline and independently re-computed for verification. [P] 3. Results
[P] 3.1. Sample characteristics
[P] The 71,867 participants contributed 694,305 person-years (median follow-up 9 years, IQR 6–14) and 11,185 incident dementia cases (15.6 %; incidence density 16.1 per 1,000 person-years). Mean age was 64.0 years and 44.1 % were male (Table 1). Half (50.5 %) had no geriatric syndrome at baseline, 31.5 % had one, 13.5 % had two and 4.6 % had all three. Every characteristic examined varied monotonically across the DAI strata: mean age rose from 62.2 to 67.7 years, the mean number of chronic conditions from 0.4 to 2.7, the proportion with low education from 47.9 % to 78.9 %, and the dementia incidence density from 11.7 to 32.3 per 1,000 person-years. Standardized mean differences between the DAI = 3 and DAI = 0 strata were large for the component-related variables (3.19) and substantial for education (0.68), age (0.61) and alcohol use (−0.60), indicating that the index captures a genuinely different risk stratum rather than a re-labelling of age. The event proportion and the incidence density are of the order reported for these cohorts and for comparable harmonized ageing populations; part of the difference from clinically verified incidence is expected, because an algorithmic criterion applied at each wave is more inclusive than a clinical diagnosis and does not require confirmation at two consecutive assessments (Table S3c).

[P] 3.2. Main association and dose–response
[P] Each additional geriatric syndrome was associated with a 27 % higher risk of incident dementia in the primary model (pooled HR 1.27, 95 % CI 1.21–1.33; I² = 76.8 %), with all six cohorts individually significant and in the same direction (Table 2A; Figure 2A). Adjustment attenuated but did not remove the association: the unadjusted estimate was 1.49 (1.38–1.61), the covariate-adjusted estimate 1.27, adjustment for urban–rural residence left it at 1.27 (1.18–1.36), and further adjustment for grip strength and physical activity gave 1.21 (1.11–1.32).

[P] The categorical specification showed a strictly monotonic gradient (Table 2B; Figure 2B). The continuous coefficient should be read as a summary of that graded association rather than as an assumption of linearity: the largest step lies between one and two syndromes, so the per-syndrome hazard ratio understates the contrast between carrying none and carrying all three. [P] 3.4. Time-lag and time-varying analyses
[P] If the association were driven by pre-clinical disease, its strength should decay as the exposure and outcome are separated in time. It did not (Table 4C). Excluding the first two years of follow-up gave a pooled HR of 1.27 (1.21–1.33) — essentially the primary estimate — and no attenuation as the lag was extended. Excluding up to 12 years still gave 1.27 (1.19–1.35). A test for linear trend in the log HR across successive lag periods was not significant (P = 0.128), i.e. there was no evidence of attenuation with increasing latency. Because successive lag estimates are built on progressively smaller and heavily overlapping subsamples, between-cohort heterogeneity varies erratically across lags (I² 25–77 %) and is not informative about attenuation; the trend test, which uses within-lag precision, is the appropriate summary. An equivalence check reinforces the point: the ratio of every lag-specific estimate to the no-lag estimate lay between 0.926 and 1.088, and the 95 % confidence interval fell entirely within ±10 % of unity at every lag (Table S10). The interval design gives the same answer. The number of participants, events and deaths contributing to each lag, together with their mean age and disability prevalence, is given in Table S12.

[P] 3.5. Exposure trajectories and reversibility
[P] Exposure trajectory was associated with dementia in a strictly ordered fashion (Figure 3; Table 4C). Across cohorts the dementia incidence density per 1,000 person-years ranged from 6.4 to 18.5 in the persistently low group and from 16.7 to 30.7 in the persistently affected group, with the remitted and incident groups lying between (Table S7). Against persistently low as the single reference, pooled HRs were 1.33 (1.22–1.47) for incident burden, 1.39 (1.26–1.54) for remitted burden and 1.62 (1.40–1.87) for persistent burden. Two features are worth noting. First, participants whose burden remitted did not return to the risk level of the persistently low group; their risk was close to that of participants with newly incident burden. Second, the baseline and the follow-up levels were each associated with dementia when entered separately (baseline level HR 1.30 (1.23–1.38); follow-up level HR 1.33 (1.23–1.44)), and both retained an independent association when entered simultaneously (1.14, 1.10–1.17 and 1.23, 1.14–1.33, respectively). The change term cannot be entered in the same model as the follow-up level because the two are collinear by construction; evaluated on its own its association was marginal (HR 1.06 (1.01–1.11)) and an order of magnitude smaller than either level term. Risk therefore tracks the level at which a person currently sits rather than the speed at which they are deteriorating.

[P] 3.7. Competing risks, sensitivity and heterogeneity
[P] Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard analysis yielded a subdistribution HR of 1.23 (1.17–1.29), consistent in direction with the cause-specific estimate. Cumulative incidence functions at each cohort's maximum follow-up (6–10 years) showed a monotonic gradient in all six cohorts, with the absolute difference between the three-syndrome and the no-syndrome groups ranging from +10.3 to +23.2 percentage points. Death is a substantial competing event in these cohorts and is itself patterned by exposure: each additional syndrome raised the hazard of death by 1.34 (1.27–1.41) (I² = 79.1 %), and the category carrying multimorbidity alone had a 52 % higher death rate than participants with none of the three syndromes (1.52 (1.45–1.59); I² = 0.0 %). Because that group is depleted by death before dementia can be ascertained, its cause-specific dementia hazard (0.89 (0.81–0.98)) is depressed; the corresponding subdistribution estimate, which retains participants who die in the risk set, was 1.05 (0.94–1.16) and no longer lay below unity, so the apparent protection is an artefact of differential depletion by death. The primary analysis remains the cause-specific Cox model reported in Table 2A; the competing-risk models address the same question under a different weighting of deaths and are reported as secondary analyses. Category-specific subdistribution estimates for the remaining combinations were unstable — the strata are sparse and between-cohort heterogeneity reached I² = 98.8 % — so they are reported for completeness in Table S14 and are not used here to rank the individual syndromes.
[P] Alternative outcome definitions produced estimates from 1.19 to 1.34, all in the same direction (Table 4D). The first three rows are estimated on the same participants and under the same follow-up rule as the primary analysis, so they differ only in how dementia was defined; the row for the education-stratified criterion reproduces Table 2A exactly. The published cohort-specific classification, available in only three cohorts and therefore estimated on a smaller sample, gave the largest estimate (1.34 (1.29–1.39)) and the alternative 25-point rule the smallest (1.19 (1.14–1.25), available in all six cohorts); the non-education-stratified algorithmic criterion gave 1.26 (1.20–1.33). A conservative rule censoring every participant at their last available cognitive assessment, which keeps the same participants but changes the follow-up rule rather than the outcome definition, gave 1.28 (1.21–1.35). Leave-one-cohort-out analysis gave pooled HRs from 1.25 to 1.29, all with P < 10⁻¹², indicating that no single cohort drove the result. The primary criterion classifies a participant as affected at the first wave at which the algorithmic threshold is crossed; because a single low score may reflect transient performance rather than incident dementia, we repeated the primary model requiring that the threshold be crossed at two consecutive cognitive assessments. Under that stricter outcome the pooled hazard ratio was 1.21 (1.13–1.30) (I² = 46.6 %; k = 5; 2,372 confirmed events), that is, attenuated but clearly positive. Requiring confirmation removes roughly three-quarters of the events, so the residual estimate is less precise, but the direction and significance of the association are unchanged.

An unmeasured confounder would therefore need to be associated with both exposure and outcome by a risk ratio of at least 1.71 — and would need to do so in addition to the nine covariates already adjusted for — to reduce the lower confidence limit to the null.
[P] Region-level pooled estimates were 1.27 (1.02–1.58) for East Asia, 1.27 (1.23–1.32) for Europe and 1.28 (1.24–1.33) for North America, with no evidence of regional difference (Q test P = 0.969). Because the pooled estimate rests on only six cohorts, we examined the sensitivity of the random-effects summary to the estimator and to the presumed heterogeneity structure. Substituting restricted maximum likelihood for the DerSimonian–Laird moment estimator changed the pooled HR from 1.27 (1.21–1.33) to 1.27 (1.21–1.33), and applying the Knapp–Hartung variance correction left it at 1.27 (1.21–1.33); the 95 % prediction interval, which describes the range in which the true cohort-specific effect would be expected to fall, was 1.097–1.463. Random-effects meta-regression found no association between the cohort-specific log hazard ratio and any of four candidate sources of heterogeneity — baseline year, median follow-up, maximum follow-up, or the event proportion (all P = 0.507–0.922). Between-cohort heterogeneity is therefore not explained by calendar period, follow-up length or case mix, and the pooled estimate is not an artefact of the particular pooling method.
[P] 3.8. Subgroups and effect modification
[P] The association was present in every stratum examined (Figure 4; Table S5); across the 15 strata the smallest pooled hazard ratio was 1.19, and no stratum estimate fell below unity or reversed direction.

[P] Sex, education and body mass index showed no material modification. Interaction was also assessed on the additive scale through the counterfactual decomposition: the mean relative excess risk due to interaction was 0.101 (range 0.026 to 0.180 across cohorts), the attributable proportion was 5.4 % (range 1.9 to 7.8 %), and the ratio of the joint risk ratio to the product of the two single-exposure risk ratios was 0.997 (range 0.993 to 0.998). These additive-scale measures likewise showed no material interaction, although they are functions of a parametric counterfactual model and are estimated less precisely than the ratio-scale tests. The estimates for women (1.22) and men (1.28) were indistinguishable (P = 0.908); education showed no gradient across low, medium and high categories (1.27, 1.30, 1.29; P = 0.17); and the BMI interaction was not significant (P = 0.30). Those models are shown for completeness in Table S8 but are not used. The quartile-indicator specification keeps all six cohorts estimable, gives 1.20 in MHAS, and is the version quoted above.
[P] Missing-data sensitivity analyses reproduced the primary estimate. Imputing covariates alone gave 1.271 (1.206–1.340) and imputing covariates together with the exposure components gave 1.271 (1.206–1.340), against 1.267 (1.207–1.330) in the complete-case analysis; the three analyses agree to within 0.0043 on the hazard-ratio scale (Table S4c). Both imputation schemes used the full analytic sample of 71,867 participants — that is, they recovered the 5,102 participants with an incomplete covariate set — without altering the estimate.
[P] 4. Discussion
[P] In 71,867 older adults from six cohorts on three continents, the cumulative burden of three common geriatric syndromes was associated with incident dementia in a dose-dependent manner, with the three-syndrome group at 64 % higher risk than those with none. The association was robust to designs that specifically target reverse causation: the estimate was unchanged when the first two years of follow-up were excluded, it showed no attenuation as the lag between exposure and outcome was extended, and when exposure was measured four to six years before outcome assessment the pooled estimate closely reproduced the baseline value. Roughly one-sixth of the effect of non-depressive burden operated through depression, a proportion that was consistent across three independent identification frameworks.
[P] 4.1. Comparison with previous work
[P] Our results are concordant with, and extend, the frailty-trajectory literature. Ward and colleagues, using four US and UK cohorts, found that frailty remained associated with dementia when measured outside the pre-dementia acceleration window, with adjusted HRs of 1.18 to 1.43 [5]; Luo and colleagues reported that frailty progression raised and frailty reversal lowered dementia risk [6]. Three further multicohort studies have appeared since. Zhou and colleagues pooled three cohorts in a harmonized individual-participant analysis and found that impairment across five intrinsic-capacity domains was associated with incident dementia in a graded fashion [39]. Wang and colleagues [40] and Peng and colleagues [41] showed, in three and in five cohorts respectively, that baseline frailty, its two-wave change and its trajectories are associated with subsequent cognitive decline and impairment; neither examined dementia incidence under a time-lagged design. The present analysis differs from all three in the exposure — three named syndromes counted equally, each of which is collected as part of the core instrument of every harmonized ageing survey, rather than a deficit-accumulation frailty index or a five-domain composite — and in the design: the association survives a 4–6-year exposure–outcome separation, and the minimal count index loses no discrimination relative to a full multivariate specification of the same components (ΔC = −0.0016), so a weighted or domain-based composite [39] is not required to capture the association. Our continuous and time-lagged point estimates (1.27 and 1.33) fall within that range; the three-syndrome contrast is necessarily higher (1.64) because it compares the extremes of the gradient rather than one-unit increments. The distinction is one of construct and of accessibility. Frailty indices of that kind are built from 30 or more deficits, and physical frailty phenotypes require grip strength and gait speed — measurements that are missing in the large majority of our cohorts. The present index requires three items that every harmonized ageing survey already collects as part of its core instrument, and we demonstrated that a fully multivariate specification of the same three components performs no better (ΔC = −0.0016). For risk stratification in settings without research-grade measurement infrastructure, that equivalence is the practically relevant finding.
[P] Directly comparable joint-exposure analyses exist, and it is worth setting out where the present results agree with them and where they do not. Du and colleagues, pooling HRS and SHARE (14,482 participants), found that older adults with both a physical disorder and depressive symptoms had a dementia hazard ratio of 2.15 (1.27–3.03) relative to those with neither [14]. Ding and colleagues, pooling the UK Biobank, ELSA and HRS, found a hazard ratio of 3.23 (2.86–3.65) for the combination of physical frailty and depression [15]. Our corresponding contrast — disability and/or multimorbidity combined with depression, against neither — was 1.70 in the counterfactual risk model (5-year risk 6.10 % against 3.58 %), the same direction but appreciably smaller. Construct severity is the most likely reason: in those studies the physical component is either the presence of any one of seven reported diagnoses or a frailty phenotype, and frailty alone carried a hazard ratio of 2.55, whereas the components of our index carried 1.16 to 1.50, because the index deliberately spans the milder end of the functional and morbidity spectrum — which is precisely what makes it usable in routine assessment. Sample composition also differs: 84 % of the dementia events in Ding and colleagues' analysis came from a single volunteer cohort, whereas ours are distributed across six population-based cohorts, three of which have no counterpart in either previous study. Beyond effect size, the earlier analyses and ours answer different questions: neither previous study examines whether the association survives several years of separation between exposure and outcome, reports it on an absolute-risk scale, or decomposes it into direct and mediated components.
[P] The component ordering — depression and disability similarly strong, and both well above multimorbidity — is consistent with the meta-analytic literature. Pooled estimates for late-life depression and incident all-cause dementia are consistently positive and moderate in magnitude, varying with the depression measure and ascertainment method [10,11]; our 1.50 sits at the conservative end. Multimorbidity meta-analyses report pooled hazard ratios of 1.34 (1.08–1.68) and 1.53 (1.12–2.09), both larger than our 1.16 [8,9]; the difference is likely to reflect both the number of conditions counted and, importantly, the fact that those analyses did not simultaneously adjust for functional limitation. Our mutually adjusted model showed that multimorbidity's association disappeared once disability and depression were accounted for, which suggests that the multimorbidity signal reported elsewhere may in part be a proxy for functional and affective burden.
[P] The eight-category analysis adds a finding that, to our knowledge, has not been reported in this form. The joint association of physical and psychological burden as an aggregate has of course been reported — in two cohorts by Du and colleagues and in three by Ding and colleagues [14,15] — but the exposure has not previously been decomposed into all eight combinations of three named syndromes, which is what allows the marginal contribution of each syndrome to be read off. Within that decomposition, "disability plus depression" (1.49) approaches all three syndromes (1.64), and multimorbidity adds little once disability and depression are present; in cause-specific analysis the category defined by multimorbidity alone appeared mildly protective (0.89 (0.81–0.98)), but this was an artefact of differential depletion by death — the corresponding subdistribution estimate, which keeps participants who die in the risk set, was 1.05 (0.94–1.16), consistent with no excess risk rather than with protection. This has a direct screening implication — a three-item assessment, of which the two most informative items are functional limitation and depressive symptoms, captures most of the gradient.
[P] 4.2. Interpretation
[P] Several non-exclusive mechanisms could underlie the association. Shared pathophysiology is the most parsimonious: vascular risk factors, chronic low-grade inflammation and reduced cognitive reserve are each implicated in disability, multimorbidity and depression, and each is independently linked to neurodegeneration. The finding that the association did not attenuate across a 4–6-year exposure–outcome separation, and showed no trend with increasing latency, is more consistent with a cumulative, long-running process than with a pre-clinical marker.
[P] Depression's role appears to be twofold. It was the strongest component when each was entered alone, and it carried a substantial portion of the physical-burden effect; in the mutually adjusted model, however, disability retained a coefficient of similar magnitude (1.36 against 1.44), so the two are best regarded as complementary rather than ordered. The four-way decomposition located most of that indirect effect in the pure indirect component rather than in an interaction (84 % of the indirect effect), indicating that depression raises dementia risk largely irrespective of the level of physical burden rather than amplifying it. At the same time, the reverse-direction control analysis gave an indirect effect of approximately the same magnitude when the exposure and mediator roles were exchanged. The mediation results must therefore be read as a temporally ordered effect decomposition rather than as directional causal mediation: the temporal ordering of the exposure, the mediator and the outcome is secured by design, but the two pathways cannot be separated in observational data of this kind.
[P] One point of divergence from the closest published analysis deserves comment. Ding and colleagues reported a statistically significant additive interaction between physical frailty and depression, with 17.1 % (6.0–28.3 %) of dementia risk in the doubly exposed group attributable to the interaction [15]. We found no material or consistent interaction on the same scale: the relative excess risk due to interaction averaged 0.101 (range 0.026 to 0.180 across cohorts), the attributable proportion 5.4 %, the synergy index 1.14 and the multiplicative ratio 0.997. This is consistent with the four-way decomposition, in which the bulk of the indirect effect lay in the pure indirect component rather than in the interaction components. The most plausible explanation is a difference in construct rather than a contradiction: the frailty phenotype identifies established multisystem physiological decline, in which the incremental effect of depression may genuinely be amplified, whereas a general geriatric-syndrome count includes milder functional and morbidity states that appear to act on largely separate pathways. Because our interaction estimate changed sign across model specifications, we do not assert that interaction is absent; we assert only that it is not a material feature of the association as measured here. Measuring both constructs in the same cohorts would settle the question.
[P] The trajectory results carry a clear clinical message. Participants whose burden remitted did not return to the risk of the persistently low group, and the change between the two assessments, which cannot be modelled jointly with the follow-up level because the two are collinear by construction, was only marginally associated with dementia when entered on its own. Risk appears to be governed by the level at which an older adult currently sits rather than by the direction in which they are moving. For clinical purposes this argues for assessing current burden status at each contact rather than for tracking trajectories.
[P] Two features of the effect-modification analyses deserve emphasis. The association was strongest in the youngest stratum (1.39 below 65 years) and declined with age, a gradient that is consistent with the accumulation of these syndromes acting within the same window as established mid-life risk factors rather than being confined to the very old (interaction HR 0.919 per 10 years, 95 % CI 0.876–0.963). This matters for screening: the index is most informative where there is still time for risk-factor modification. Two caveats attach to this gradient. First, the youngest stratum also has the fewest events and the lowest competing risk of death, whereas in the oldest stratum death removes a larger share of participants before dementia can be ascertained; part of the apparent decline in effect size with age may therefore be a competing-risk phenomenon rather than a difference in the underlying biology. Second, we cannot distinguish an effect-modifying role of age from a difference in the timing of ascertainment. We therefore present the age gradient as a descriptive pattern that is consistent with a mid-life risk window rather than as evidence of one. Marital status likewise modified the association (1.31, 1.22–1.40 with a partner versus 1.22, 1.15–1.28 without), and this contrast survived correction for multiplicity, but detection bias remains a plausible explanation that we cannot exclude: a partner may notice and report cognitive decline earlier, and the two groups may differ in the timing of death. We therefore report the marital contrast as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than as an established effect modifier. Baseline cognition did not modify the association (P = 0.40); the earlier suggestion that participants with better baseline cognition were more susceptible is therefore not supported, and the evidence against reverse causation rests on the time-lag and interval designs alone.
[P] 4.3. Strengths and limitations
[P] The principal strengths are the scale and breadth of the harmonized sample (six cohorts on three continents, including Korean and Mexican populations that are under-represented in this literature), the deliberately redundant analytic strategy across multiple designs and frameworks, and the demonstration that a minimal index loses no information relative to a full specification.
[P] Several limitations must be acknowledged. Outcome ascertainment. The outcome was an algorithmic classification of probable dementia rather than a clinical diagnosis. Agreement between our derived criterion and the classifications distributed with the harmonized files was moderate (Cohen κ 0.39–0.61 across ELSA, HRS and KLoSA) [30,31], and alternative outcome definitions gave estimates from 1.19 to 1.34, all concordant with the primary result. Exposure measurement. Exposure components are self-reported: multimorbidity reflects physician diagnosis reported by the participant, and depression is a scale threshold rather than a clinical diagnosis. Such misclassification is generally expected to bias estimates towards the null, so the true associations may be somewhat stronger. The components are equally weighted without external validation of the weights, although a first-principal-component weighted index gave a similar estimate with near-equal loadings, supporting that choice. Measurement comparability. The harmonized cognitive instruments differ between cohorts — a 27-point battery, a 25-point battery or item-level scores depending on the survey — and the criterion is a within-cohort education-stratified z-score threshold in the tradition of the Langa–Weir approach rather than a fixed cut-off on a shared scale. We did not test measurement invariance formally, so the criterion should be read as comparable in construction but not strictly equivalent across cohorts; that is also why event rates exceed those reported from clinically ascertained cohorts, since the criterion also captures milder impairment.
[P] Design and confounding. Residual confounding cannot be excluded in an observational design; the E-value of 1.71 for the lower confidence limit quantifies how strong such confounding would need to be. Follow-up length varies considerably (MHAS 6 years, CHARLS 9 years, HRS 21 years), which reduces precision in the longest-lag strata. The time-varying models showed greater between-cohort heterogeneity (I² 55–91 %) than the baseline models (76.8 %), and were further restricted by covariate completeness; they should be treated as exploratory, with the baseline models providing the primary effect estimates. Standard errors in the time-varying models were clustered by participant, which accounts for the correlation between the successive intervals contributed by one person; the cluster-robust standard errors were between 0.972 and 0.995 times the conventional ones and the point estimates were unchanged. The counterfactual mediation analysis rests on two untestable assumptions — sequential ignorability and the absence of exposure-induced confounding — and its risks are extrapolated from parametric Cox and logistic models; it is presented as a cross-validation of the mediated proportion under a different scale and framework, not as causal evidence. The mediated proportion was poolable in all six cohorts under every framework, so no specification lost a cohort, but its value depends on the pooling estimand: pooling cohort-specific proportions gives 12.7–17.4 % whereas taking the ratio of the pooled coefficients gives 18.8–18.9 %. Both are reported, and under either depression remains a quantitatively modest mediator. Age range. The index required an age of 50 years or over because the three syndromes are uncommon earlier in life; as a result few events arise in the youngest stratum, and the estimates for that group rest on far fewer outcomes than the older strata, so the age interaction should be read with that in mind.
[P] Generalisability and multiplicity. The joint association of physical and psychological burden with dementia has already been reported in two and in three cohorts [14,15]; the contribution of the present analysis therefore lies in scale, geographic breadth, temporal design and mechanism rather than in first description. The Indian cohort (LASI) could not be included because it has only a single wave, so coverage of low- and middle-income settings rests on CHARLS and MHAS. Subgroup and interaction analyses were corrected for multiplicity across the six prespecified modifiers by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and age and marital status remained significant after that correction; the interaction estimates were themselves heterogeneous across cohorts. All 15 stratum-specific estimates nevertheless remained above unity, so the qualitative conclusion does not depend on the subgroup results. Finally, 7.1 % of participants with a complete exposure lacked at least one covariate and were older and more often disabled; multiple imputation analyses that recovered them reproduced the primary estimate to within 0.0043. Coverage and heterogeneity. Coverage is uneven in two respects: Europe is represented only by Western Europe (SHARE), and the only East Asian cohorts are CHARLS and KLoSA, so these estimates should not be generalised to South Asia, Africa, or Latin American settings other than Mexico. Between-cohort heterogeneity in the primary model was substantial (I² = 76.8 %; τ² = 0.0028 on the log-hazard scale), which reflects genuine differences in cohort composition, exposure prevalence and outcome ascertainment rather than sampling error alone; the point estimate was nevertheless insensitive to the choice of heterogeneity estimator and to leave-one-cohort-out analysis.
[P] 5. Conclusions
[P] Cumulative burden of disability, multimorbidity and depression is associated with incident dementia in a robust, dose-dependent and geographically consistent manner, and the association survives designs that place the exposure several years before outcome ascertainment. Depression accounts for roughly one-sixth of the effect of physical burden, and the combination of disability with depression approaches the risk of all three syndromes combined. Because the index requires only three items that are collected in routine geriatric assessment, it offers a practical, low-cost way of identifying older adults at elevated dementia risk and supports the view that late-life depression and functional limitation are the two most informative targets within that assessment. The index is a risk-stratification aid rather than a diagnostic test: it indicates who may warrant a fuller cognitive assessment, and it carries no implication that the burden it measures is itself reversible or that modifying it would alter dementia risk. Because no disease-modifying treatment for dementia exists, the value of stratifying risk at present lies in targeting surveillance and in the management of the individual syndromes rather than in a demonstrated route to prevention.
